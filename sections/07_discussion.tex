\section{\uppercase{Discussion}}
\label{sec:discussion}

\noindent
The three experiments indicate that the classifiers use the pen dynamics encoded in the minimaps. When the static trace was available and favored by ties, training-only selection still chose a dynamic encoding in 24 of 25 training sets in Experiment~2, and in 23 of 25 with LPQ and 24 of 25 with ResNet18 in Experiment~3. Displacing the signals within strokes lowered recording-level macro F1, most under joint permutation, so the position of each value along the trajectory matters and not only the colors present. The benefit is clearest on the spiral, where $SAZ$ added 0.0802 in Experiment~1 and permutation removed 0.0609 in Experiment~2.

The gains depend on the task (Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap}). One encoding chosen for all eight tasks averages gains on the spiral, T6 and T8 with losses on T2, T3, T5 and T7, which keeps its eight-task mean close to the static trace, whereas on the homogeneous letter patterns of T2--T4 the selected encoding gives the best participant-level decisions. Choosing encodings per task, rather than one for the whole protocol, is a direct way to exploit this dependence.

The pipelines are also strong on different endpoints rather than uniformly: minimaps with LPQ give the best participant-level decisions, while the kinematic attributes lead the recording-level mean and the vote-fraction AUC. The representation works with both a handcrafted texture descriptor and pretrained convolutional features, and this complementarity between the image and kinematic pipelines motivates combining them.

\section{\uppercase{Conclusion}}
\label{sec:conclusion}
\noindent
This initial study introduced pseudodynamic minimaps, a new image representation that places pen speed, pressure, altitude and azimuth as color along the handwriting trajectory. When evaluated on the PaHaW dataset using participant-separated partitions, the representation was consistently preferred to static traces by a training-based selection procedure, displacing the dynamics within each stroke reduced its performance, and across the three repeated-pattern tasks it achieved the best participant-level results among the four pipelines compared. These results indicate that pseudodynamic minimaps are an effective way to bring pen dynamics into image-based handwriting analysis for Parkinson's disease. They rest, however, on a single dataset of 75 participants, so validation on independent cohorts is required to establish the robustness and generalizability of the observed gains.

Future work will choose encodings per task, combine minimaps with kinematic descriptors, and fine-tune pretrained networks on the minimaps. A fair comparison with other published results on the PaHaW dataset is also left for future work.
