\keywords{Handwriting Analysis, Visual Signatures, Signal-to-Image Encoding, Pseudo-Dynamic
Minimaps, Representation Learning, Online Handwriting, Parkinson's Disease.}

\abstract{Online handwriting from a digitizing tablet carries dynamic information --- pen
pressure, altitude, azimuth, speed and pen-up trajectories --- that a binary rasterization
discards. Recovering it usually needs handcrafted kinematic descriptors or sequence models,
neither of which lets the computer vision toolchain be reused. We propose \emph{Pseudodynamic
Handwriting Minimap Signatures}, an encoding that maps the signal onto a single static RGB
raster whose colour channels carry pen attributes and whose stroke width varies with writing
speed. It is deterministic, versioned and normalized per task without reference to
class labels. We generate 597 minimaps from the 75 PaHaW subjects and evaluate them under
repeated subject-grouped cross-validation. On three tasks, a $2\times2$ factorial ablation
isolates the pseudo-dynamic contribution: the full encoding averages 0.6901 subject-level macro
F1 over repeats, 0.7321 pooled, against 0.5597 for a position-only rendering. The paired gain of
0.1725 survives Holm correction. A fine-tuned ResNet-18 performed worse than standard texture
descriptors. Overall the representation stays below the handcrafted kinematic and pressure
pipeline reported for this database, but on the Archimedean spiral, the one task for which that
pipeline reports no significant kinematic feature, it reaches 0.719 balanced accuracy against
62.8\%. This is exploratory research software, not a diagnostic system.}

\section{\uppercase{Introduction}}
\label{sec:introduction}
\noindent
Handwriting is acquired in two regimes. \emph{Offline} acquisition yields a static image: a
scanned page, a photograph, a binary raster of ink. \emph{Online} acquisition, on a digitizing
tablet or with an instrumented pen, yields a time-ordered sequence of samples in which every
point carries planar coordinates together with the pressure exerted on the surface, the
inclination and rotation of the pen, and a velocity implicit in consecutive timestamps. Those
extra channels are the ones that record motor control rather than orthography.

The richer signal creates a representation problem. A computer vision pipeline expects an
image; an online handwriting signal is a multivariate time series. Two reconciliations are
common. One discards the temporal dimension and rasterizes the trajectory into a binary image,
throwing away the very attributes that motivated online acquisition. The other summarizes the
signal into handcrafted kinematic and pressure descriptors --- stroke duration, number of
velocity changes, pressure statistics --- and feeds the resulting vector to a conventional
classifier \cite{Drotar2016,Impedovo2019}. Dynamic information survives the second route, but
at the price of feature engineering: descriptors have to be designed and then redesigned for
each new task or acquisition device.

A third route re-encodes the dynamics \emph{into} the image, making the image itself their
carrier. \emph{Pseudo\-dynamic} representations follow this idea, and one such encoding is what
we propose here. A \emph{Pseudodynamic Handwriting Minimap Signature} renders an online
handwriting task as a single static RGB raster whose three colour channels transport pen
pressure, altitude and azimuth, and whose stroke width varies with the instantaneous writing
speed. Pen-up movements, invisible in a conventionally rasterized sample and known to be
informative \cite{Diaz2019}, appear as dashed light-gray segments. The output is an ordinary
image file, readable by texture descriptors feeding a linear model \cite{Cortes1995} or by a
convolutional network \cite{LeCun1998,He2016} without further preparation.

Posing the task as image classification buys more than convenience. Decades of texture
descriptors, convolutional architectures, attribution methods and evaluation practice become
usable without a line of signal-specific code. A representation defined on pixels also survives
changes of acquisition device, sampling rate or task inventory, which a kinematic descriptor set
typically does not, and it opens the door to models pretrained on large natural-image corpora.
Finally, an image can be looked at, and a feature vector cannot: a clinician or a reviewer can
inspect exactly what the model consumed and judge whether the encoding is tracking motor
behaviour or an artifact. Section~\ref{sec:tool} builds on that last property.

The term \emph{minimap} comes from work applying the same principle in a very different domain.
Gebara Jr. et al. showed that the zoomed-out rendering of a source file behaves as a
machine-readable signature from which software properties can be predicted \cite{Gebara2025},
and later deployed the idea as a multi-IDE analysis infrastructure \cite{Gebara2026}. Those
minimaps are grayscale renderings of static text. We extend the principle along two axes at
once: the source becomes a temporally sampled signal, and the encoding becomes multi-channel
and semantic, each channel bound to a named physical attribute of the pen.

The central hypothesis under test is the following:

\begin{quote}
Pseudodynamic Handwriting Minimap Signatures are a valid representation of online handwriting for
computer vision classifiers.
\end{quote}

\noindent Validity is meant here in a modest sense: not that the encoding is optimal, nor that
it is lossless in an information-theoretic sense, but that the pseudo-dynamic content survives
the transformation and is measurably used by generic image classifiers. The claim is
falsifiable, and we test it with a $2\times2$ factorial ablation that switches the semantic
colour channels and the speed-driven stroke width on and off independently, holding descriptor,
classifier, cross-validation splits and decision threshold fixed.

The contributions of this paper are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A formal, versioned and reproducible encoding, Minimap Encoding V2, that maps an online
      handwriting signal to a static RGB image using label-agnostic per-task robust
      normalization (Section~\ref{sec:minimaps}).
\item A validation protocol establishing that the encoding is deterministic and numerically
      equivalent across CPU and GPU implementations (Section~\ref{sec:validation}).
\item A factorial ablation quantifying how much of the classification signal is attributable to
      the pseudo-dynamic content rather than to trajectory geometry
      (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
\item An interactive visualization tool that replays the original task execution and exposes its
      mapping to the minimap, including synchronized side-by-side comparison of two subjects on
      the same task (Section~\ref{sec:tool}).
\item A comparison between standard texture descriptors and a fine-tuned convolutional network
      on this representation, in which the network loses (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:related} positions the work. Section~\ref{sec:minimaps} specifies the encoding
and the inspection tool. Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the experimental protocol,
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports results, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses them, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{\uppercase{Related Work}}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Minimaps as Visual Signatures}
\noindent
The immediate precedent treats a rendered artifact not as a visualization for a human reader
but as a machine-readable signature for a model. Gebara Jr. et al. generated minimaps of source
files, compact and essentially illegible zoomed-out renderings of the text, and showed that
convolutional models can detect software features in them without tokenization or syntactic
parsing \cite{Gebara2025}. The follow-up packaged the approach as IDE plugins over a decoupled
inference service, generating anonymized $128 \times 128$ grayscale minimaps per source file and
validating artifact type, project and author as feasibility benchmarks \cite{Gebara2026}.

CV4Code \cite{Shi2022} rests on the same conviction, encoding a code snippet as a
two-dimensional ASCII codepoint image and learning from it directly, which makes the method
language-agnostic. A visual signature, these works suggest, can be an adequate substrate for
classification even when the source domain is not visual at all.

Two properties of that prior art matter here. The source artifacts are \emph{static}: a source
file has no temporal ordering to preserve. And the encodings are \emph{grayscale}, a single
intensity dimension carrying a density or codepoint value. Online handwriting breaks the first
property and relaxes the second, since several physically distinct attributes are measured at
once and can be assigned to separate colour channels.

\subsection{Handwriting Analysis for Neurodegenerative Disease}
\noindent
Handwriting has been studied at length as a behavioural marker of neurodegenerative conditions,
Parkinson's disease above all, since bradykinesia, rigidity and tremor show up directly in fine
motor control of the pen. Impedovo and Pirlo survey the field from a pattern recognition
standpoint, organizing it around the dynamic descriptors extracted from online acquisitions
\cite{Impedovo2019}.

The reference resource in this line is the PaHaW database \cite{Drotar2016}. Its authors
extracted conventional kinematic descriptors alongside newly proposed pressure descriptors and
compared $k$-nearest neighbours, AdaBoost and support vector machines, the last performing best.
That pipeline is entirely feature-engineered: performance follows from which descriptors were
designed, not from what a model found in the signal.

Learned representations have also been tried. Pereira et al. applied convolutional networks to
signals from an instrumented pen and reported that learned features outperformed the raw signals
\cite{Pereira2016}. Moetesum et al. went the other way, quantifying the discriminative power of
\emph{visual} attributes of graphomotor samples with convolutional feature extractors
\cite{Moetesum2019}.

Closest to the present proposal is the work of Diaz et al. on \emph{dynamically enhanced} static
handwriting images \cite{Diaz2019}. Their representation retains temporal and velocity
information by drawing sample points rather than linking them and by adding pen-up strokes, and
it outperformed purely static and purely dynamic baselines on PaHaW. Our motivation is the same;
the mechanism differs. We do not use point density as an implicit velocity proxy. Each pen
attribute goes to an explicitly named channel under a documented scaling rule, and speed is
rendered through a bounded stroke-width function rather than through sample sparsity. That is
what makes the ablation of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} possible at all, since each encoded
attribute can then be switched off on its own.

\subsection{Signal-to-Image Encodings}
\noindent
Outside handwriting, converting a time series into an image in order to apply convolutional
models is an established strategy. Recurrence plots, introduced by Eckmann et al. to visualize
the recurrence structure of dynamical systems \cite{Eckmann1987}, were later used directly as
classifier inputs \cite{Hatami2018}. Wang and Oates proposed Gramian angular fields and Markov
transition fields, encoding temporal correlation and transition dynamics as image texture, and
showed competitive results across standard benchmarks \cite{Wang2015}.

Those encodings are \emph{abstract}: the image is a matrix of pairwise relations bearing no
spatial correspondence to the phenomenon that produced the signal. A recurrence plot of a
handwriting task does not look like handwriting. Minimaps take the opposite stance, preserving
the planar geometry of the trace and spending the remaining degrees of freedom, colour and
stroke width, on the non-spatial attributes. The image therefore stays recognizable, and a human
expert can still inspect it and reason about it --- which matters when the encoding has to be
trusted in a sensitive domain.

\subsection{Positioning}
\noindent
Minimaps, then, have been validated as visual signatures for static textual artifacts under a
grayscale encoding; signal-to-image methods for time series under encodings that discard spatial
correspondence; handwriting-specific pseudo-dynamic images under encodings that embed dynamics
implicitly. We are not aware of a representation that is at once spatially faithful,
multi-attribute, semantically explicit and formally specified, nor of a study isolating each
encoded attribute experimentally.

\section{\uppercase{Pseudodynamic Minimap Signatures}}
\label{sec:minimaps}

\subsection{Input Signal}
\noindent
The input is an online handwriting task recorded as a sequence of $N$ samples
$p_i = (x_i, y_i, t_i, s_i, \alpha_i, \beta_i, \pi_i)$, $i = 1 \ldots N$, where $(x_i, y_i)$ are
planar coordinates, $t_i$ is a timestamp in milliseconds, $s_i \in \{0,1\}$ indicates whether
the pen is on the surface, $\alpha_i$ is the azimuth, $\beta_i$ is the altitude and $\pi_i$ is
the pressure. Consecutive samples define segments; the encoding is defined per segment.

\subsection{Design Rationale}
\noindent
The design answers to four requirements. \emph{Spatial fidelity}: the rendered trace stays
geometrically faithful to the trajectory, so the image remains recognizable as handwriting and
interpretable by a human. \emph{Channel disjointness}: every measured attribute occupies exactly
one visual dimension, and no visual dimension carries two attributes. An anomaly in the red
channel can then only have come from pressure, which is what makes both auditing and the
ablation of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} possible. \emph{Separability from the background}: no
semantic value may be confused with the empty canvas. \emph{Label independence}: nothing used to
build the image may depend on the class label of any sample, or the representation leaks
supervision into the evaluation downstream.

Table~\ref{tab:encoding} summarizes the assignment, and Figure~\ref{fig:minimaps} shows the
result on one control and one patient performing the same task. Two things the encoding deliberately avoids
are worth naming, since earlier iterations did both and made channel-level attribution
impossible: writing speed into a colour channel, and letting pressure modulate stroke width.

\begin{table}[h]
\caption{Minimap Encoding V2: assignment of pen attributes to visual dimensions.}
\label{tab:encoding} \centering
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|}
  \hline
  Visual element & Encoded attribute \\
  \hline
  Red channel & Pressure \\
  Green channel & Altitude \\
  Blue channel & Azimuth \\
  Stroke width & Writing speed (Eq.~\ref{eq:width}) \\
  Width range & 1--8 px \\
  Background & White, RGB (255, 255, 255) \\
  Pen-up segments & Light-gray dashed line \\
  \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Semantic Range}
\noindent
The three semantic channels map into $[30, 220]$ rather than the full $[0,255]$ range, with the
background left pure white at $(255,255,255)$. No semantic pixel is then numerically confusable
with the canvas, and extreme attribute values saturate into neither the background nor pure
black. Roughly a quarter of the usable dynamic range is lost, which costs little: the source
attributes are quantized far more coarsely than 190 levels.

\subsection{Speed to Stroke Width}
\noindent
For a segment between samples $i$ and $i+1$, the instantaneous speed is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:speed}
v_i = \frac{\sqrt{(x_{i+1}-x_i)^2 + (y_{i+1}-y_i)^2}}{t_{i+1}-t_i} ,
\end{equation}

\noindent compressed logarithmically as $\tilde{v}_i = \log(1+v_i)$. Only strictly positive time
differences are used. Real acquisitions contain invalid, repeated and non-monotonic timestamps;
these fall back deterministically to the previous valid speed, or to the median valid speed of
the file when no previous one exists. The rule is fixed and stateless with respect to input
order, which is what keeps the pipeline reproducible.

Normalization is computed per task over all selected files, and never per class:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:speednorm}
\hat{v}_i = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{\tilde{v}_i - q_{5}}{\max(q_{95} - q_{5}, \epsilon)},\, 0,\, 1\right),
\end{equation}

\noindent where $q_{5}$ and $q_{95}$ are the $5$th and $95$th percentiles of $\tilde{v}$ over the
task. The stroke width in pixels is then

\begin{equation}\label{eq:width}
w_i = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\mathrm{round}\!\left(8 - 7\hat{v}_i\right),\, 1,\, 8\right).
\end{equation}

\noindent Equation~\ref{eq:width} decreases monotonically in the normalized writing speed
$\hat{v}_i$: width is driven by speed and shrinks as the pen moves faster. Speeds at or below the
$5$th percentile render at 8 px, speeds at or above the $95$th at 1 px, zero speed at 8 px after
clipping, and everything between at 2--7 px. Slow movement thus reads as a thick stroke, which
matches the intuition of a hesitant or effortful trace.

\subsection{Pressure, Altitude and Azimuth}
\noindent
Pressure is scaled per task using pen-down samples only, since pressure recorded while the pen
is in the air carries no meaning:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:red}
R_i = \mathrm{round}\!\left(30 + 190\,\mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{\pi_i - \pi_{q5}}{\max(\pi_{q95}-\pi_{q5}, \epsilon)},0,1\right)\right).
\end{equation}

\noindent Altitude and azimuth go to the green and blue channels under the same task-level
percentile scaling and into the same $[30,220]$ interval. Invalid values are excluded when the
profile is computed and clipped at render time, so a corrupt sample distorts at most one segment
and never the profile of a whole task.

\subsection{Pen-Up Segments}
\noindent
Segments with $s_i = 0$ are movements executed above the surface, rendered as light-gray dashed
lines. Their pressure is meaningless and is not interpreted, but their width still follows
Equation~\ref{eq:width}, keeping the temporal structure of the aerial movement visible. We retain
pen-up trajectories because they carry discriminative content \cite{Diaz2019}.

\subsection{Rendering Procedure}
\noindent
Algorithm~\ref{alg:render} states the procedure. Label independence is enforced by the two-pass
structure: the profile is computed first, from the task rather than from the labels, and only
then is the image rendered.

\begin{algorithm}[!h]
 \caption{Minimap Encoding V2 rendering.}
 \label{alg:render}
 \KwData{Samples $p_1 \ldots p_N$ of a task; task-level profile $P$}
 \KwResult{RGB minimap $M$}
 initialize $M$ to white RGB (255,255,255)\;
 \For{$i \leftarrow 1$ \KwTo $N-1$}{
   compute $v_i$ by Eq.~\ref{eq:speed}, applying the fallback rule if $t_{i+1} \le t_i$\;
   $w_i \leftarrow$ width by Eq.~\ref{eq:speednorm}--\ref{eq:width} using $P$\;
   \eIf{$s_i = 1$}{
     $R_i \leftarrow$ Eq.~\ref{eq:red}; $G_i, B_i \leftarrow$ altitude, azimuth scaled by $P$\;
     draw solid segment $(x_i,y_i)\!\rightarrow\!(x_{i+1},y_{i+1})$ with colour $(R_i,G_i,B_i)$ and width $w_i$\;
   }{
     draw dashed light-gray segment with width $w_i$\;
   }
 }
 \Return $M$\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Determinism and Numerical Validation}
\label{sec:validation}
\noindent
The encoding is meant as a specification rather than an implementation detail, so it lives in a
versioned machine-readable configuration, and every generated minimap carries a manifest with
the configuration version, the source file and a SHA-256 checksum of the output.

Generation of the 597 minimaps used here (Section~\ref{sec:setup}) was validated automatically.
All 597 images passed the structural audit: no broken file, no residual reference to an earlier
encoding version, and stroke widths spanning exactly the specified 1 to 8 pixels. CPU and CUDA
implementations were compared segment by segment on 80 task files, covering all eight tasks for
ten subjects. Rendered RGB values differed by exactly $0$, normalized speeds by at most $2.22 \times 10^{-16}$, one unit in
the last place of double precision, and stroke widths matched on every segment. Equivalence holds
at the level of the rendered pixels, which is the level the classifiers see.

\subsection{Visual Inspection Tool}
\label{sec:tool}
\noindent
A static specification is hard to audit. We therefore built a web-based viewer, which is also
what allowed the encoding to be designed and debugged in the first place.

Figure~\ref{fig:animated} shows the single-subject mode. A subject is selected, and the tool
replays each task of that subject as it was originally executed, incrementally rendering the
trace at $1\times$, $2\times$, $4\times$ or $8\times$ playback speed while displaying the point
count and the duration of each task. A pen-orientation indicator shows altitude and azimuth as
they evolve, which makes it possible to confirm visually that the green and blue channels track
the physical inclination of the pen rather than an artifact of the scaling.

Figure~\ref{fig:sidebyside} shows the comparison mode, replaying two subjects on the \emph{same}
task synchronously. Here the discriminative content becomes apparent to a human observer: the
two Archimedean spirals are geometrically similar, yet they differ in trace regularity and in
the speed profile that the colour gradient exposes.

The viewer also displays subject metadata --- age, sex, dominant hand, UPDRS-V score, levodopa
equivalent dose, disease duration. None of it reaches any model. As Section~\ref{sec:setup}
states, the classifiers consume minimap images, subject identifiers, task identifiers and
labels, and nothing else; the metadata is there for human inspection and dataset auditing.

The tool is a research instrument, not a clinical interface, and is not intended for patient
assessment.

\section{\uppercase{Experimental Setup}}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Database and Generated Corpus}
\noindent
Experiments use the PaHaW database \cite{Drotar2016}, which contains online handwriting from 75
subjects --- 37 diagnosed with Parkinson's disease and 38 healthy controls --- each performing
eight tasks on a digitizing tablet. The tasks comprise an Archimedean spiral, repeated writing
of orthographically simple syllables and words, and the writing of a sentence. Access to PaHaW
requires a signed licence agreement; no data is redistributed with this work.

Each task is stored as an \texttt{.svc} file whose first line is the sample count and whose
remaining lines contain seven columns in the order

\begin{small}
\begin{verbatim}
 y  x  timestamp  state  azimuth  altitude
 pressure
\end{verbatim}
\end{small}

\noindent with \texttt{state} equal to 1 for pen-on-surface and 0 for pen-in-air. The $y$
coordinate precedes $x$, an unusual ordering that regularly breaks reproductions written under
the more common $(x,y)$ assumption.

Generation under encoding version 2.0 produced \textbf{597 minimaps} of $512 \times 512$ pixels
in RGB, with no file skipped and no failure. Coverage is complete for tasks 2 to 8, at 75 images
each; task 1 yields 72 images because three subjects have no usable recording for it.

\subsection{Information Available to the Classifiers}
\noindent
The \texttt{.svc} files serve only to render minimaps. Past that point every classifier and
every analysis here consumes four things: the minimap image, the subject identifier for
grouping, the task identifier for organization, and the H/PD label as target. No handcrafted
kinematic or pressure descriptor is extracted from the raw signal, no demographic or clinical
covariate enters, and dynamic and visual modalities are never fused. The restriction is
deliberate; it is what makes this a test of the \emph{representation} rather than of a pipeline
assembled around it.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\noindent
All splits are subject-independent. Outer evaluation uses stratified group $k$-fold with 5 folds
repeated 5 times, grouped by subject identifier, so no subject ever appears simultaneously in
training and evaluation. Model, descriptor and fusion decisions are taken inside 3 inner grouped
folds, never using outer-test subjects. The random seed is 42 and the decision threshold is
fixed at 0.50. Confidence intervals are percentile bootstrap over 10,000 subject-level
resamplings, and paired comparisons use paired subject bootstrap, paired permutation tests,
exact McNemar tests and Holm--Bonferroni correction. The frozen split checksum is
\texttt{ca8d48f4\ldots a6bf5dcf}.

The primary metric is macro F1, reported alongside balanced accuracy, per-class recall, Matthews
correlation coefficient and ROC AUC. A classifier that always predicts the majority class
attains a balanced accuracy of exactly 0.500 by construction, and that is the reference against
which the results below should be read.

\subsection{Evaluated Pipelines}
\noindent
Three pipelines were evaluated, all consuming only minimap pixels.

\textbf{Texture-descriptor screening.} A nested evaluation over all eight tasks explored ten
descriptor families --- grayscale LBP, RGB semantic-channel LBP, opponent LBP, LPQ,
GLCM/Haralick, Gabor, HOG, wavelet texture, semantic channel statistics, and raster-derived
stroke-width texture --- against logistic regression, linear and RBF support vector machines
\cite{Cortes1995}, random forests and extra trees. Descriptor, classifier and task-fusion
settings were chosen inside inner folds only.

\textbf{Fixed descriptor with reliability-weighted fusion.} The configuration retained from
screening, denoted \texttt{lpq\_rgb\_visual\_geometry\_width}, concatenates LPQ computed
independently on the R, G and B channels (768 features, window size 5, Hellinger-transformed
histograms, after fixed foreground crop, 5\% margin, white padding and $64 \times 64$ bilinear
resize) with 44 visual geometry and stroke-width features computed on the unresized PNG, for 812
features in total. The classifier is a fixed pipeline of median imputation, standardization and
logistic regression with $C = 0.1$, L2 penalty, balanced class weights and the liblinear solver.
Per-task predictions for tasks 1, 3 and 7 are fused with reliability weights
$\max(\text{inner macro F1} - 0.5,\, 0.01)$, renormalized over the tasks available for each
subject.

\textbf{Direct convolutional classification.} A ResNet-18 \cite{He2016} is fine-tuned on raw
minimap pixels under identical splits, class weighting, threshold and fusion rule. The RGB
channels being semantic, the augmentations normally applied to natural images --- rotation,
scaling, blur, colour jitter, crops, flips, erasing, MixUp, CutMix --- would corrupt the encoded
attributes, and all are excluded. The one train-time augmentation is a safe canvas translation
on the $512 \times 512$ raster before resizing: integer-pixel copy with white fill, no
interpolation, at most 16 pixels of shift per axis, probability 0.80, foreground clipping
forbidden. An audit of 1046 training-time transformations recorded 851 translated and 195
identity samples, zero clipped and zero foreground-preservation failures.

\subsection{Factorial Ablation}
\label{sec:ablation-setup}
\noindent
Four rendering variants were generated from the same signals of tasks 1, 3 and 7 in a
$2\times2$ design crossing semantic colour with speed-driven stroke width, giving 222 images per
variant and 888 in total. \textsc{Mono\_Fixed} keeps position, trajectory
and pen contact as a monochrome fixed-width line; \textsc{Rgb\_Fixed} adds the semantic colour
channels; \textsc{Mono\_Speed} adds speed-driven stroke width; \textsc{Rgb\_Speed\_V2} is the
complete encoding. Descriptor, classifier, splits and threshold stay fixed across all four, so any difference
between variants is attributable to the rendering. Pen contact is present in every
variant, the minimal one included.

\subsection{Texture Descriptors across All Eight Tasks}

\subsection{Convolutional Network on the Same Representation}
\noindent
The fine-tuned ResNet-18 with safe translation reaches 0.5601 macro F1 at the ensemble level
(95\% CI [0.4444, 0.6701]), balanced accuracy 0.5711, PD recall 0.4054, MCC 0.1509 and ROC AUC
0.6024. On task 1 it reaches 0.5625 macro F1 with a strongly asymmetric operating point: H
recall 0.9444 against PD recall 0.2778.

Paired against the texture pipeline on the same subjects and splits, the convolutional model is
\emph{worse}: $\Delta = -0.1720$ at the ensemble level (95\% CI [$-0.294$, $-0.054$],
permutation $p = 0.010$, exact McNemar $p = 0.017$, Holm-adjusted $p = 0.040$). On task 1 the
gap is significant before correction ($\Delta = -0.1711$, permutation $p = 0.033$); on tasks 3
and 7 it is not. We report the negative result as it stands. At this cohort size, standard
texture descriptors extract the pseudo-dynamic content of the minimaps better than end-to-end
fine-tuning does.

\subsection{Relation to a Clipping-Based Pipeline}
\noindent
For context, a convolutional pipeline that operates on binary stroke clippings rather than on a
pseudo-dynamic encoding \needcite{the clipping-based CNN baseline; it currently exists only as an
unpublished undergraduate thesis and an accompanying public repository, so an appropriate
citable form must be decided} reports 69.23\% task-level accuracy and 66.67\% F1 on PaHaW task 2
with 62 training and 13 test subjects. That pipeline extracts one square clipping per coordinate
point --- roughly 83{,}955 clippings for the task --- and aggregates clipping-level predictions
upward into stroke-, letter- and task-level decisions.

The two sets of figures are not directly comparable: the protocols differ in task, metric and
split design, and one held-out split of 13 subjects moves by 7.7 accuracy points per subject
against 25 subject-grouped rounds here. We cite that pipeline to characterize the regime, not to
outrank it. One property does bear recording: its clipping-level test accuracy stayed in the
50--52\% band throughout training while training accuracy reached 96.8\%, the expected
consequence of treating tens of thousands of overlapping crops from a few dozen writers as
independent samples.

\section{\uppercase{Discussion}}
\label{sec:discussion}

\noindent
\textbf{The encoding could have failed the ablation, and did not.} Had the colour channels and
the speed-driven width carried nothing, the minimal position-only rendering would have matched
the full one. \textsc{Mono\_Fixed} instead sits at 0.5597 macro F1, barely above the 0.500
balanced accuracy of a trivial classifier, against 0.7321 for the complete encoding. The gain is
paired at the subject level, survives Holm correction, and has a confidence interval excluding
zero. Descriptor, classifier, splits and threshold were frozen across the four variants, which
leaves the representation as the only source of the difference.

\textbf{Neither factor is sufficient alone.} No main effect reached significance after
correction, yet the combination did, and each factor is worth roughly twice as much in the
presence of the other. The pattern is consistent with the two dimensions being
complementary rather than redundant. One reading, which these data support but do not establish,
is that stroke width localizes \emph{where} the movement slowed while the colour channels
describe \emph{how the pen was held and pressed} while it slowed. The descriptor screening is
weakly consistent with this: the only task whose selected descriptor combined colour with an
explicit stroke-width block is also the only task that exceeded chance on its own. Descriptor
selection is not evidence of isolation, so the observation carries little weight by itself.

\textbf{Why the convolutional model lost.} Both pipelines consume the same pixels, so the
negative CNN result says nothing against the representation. It says something about sample
size. A fine-tuned ResNet-18 has orders of magnitude more parameters than 597 images from 75
subjects can constrain, and the semantic RGB channels rule out every standard photometric and
geometric augmentation. Its operating point on task 1 shows the failure mode plainly: 0.944
recall on controls against 0.278 on patients, a model predicting the majority side of its own
decision boundary. The augmentation we designed --- integer translation, audited for foreground
preservation --- was conservative because anything stronger would have destroyed the encoded
attributes. The constraint is intrinsic to semantic channel encodings: the richer the semantics
of the channels, the smaller the space of admissible augmentations.

\textbf{Task heterogeneity.} Task 1, the Archimedean spiral, dominates. Being a continuous
ballistic movement with no orthographic content, executed against a fixed template, it makes
speed and pressure irregularities directly comparable across subjects. Tasks with repeated
lifts, restarts and letter shapes introduce variance the representation does not normalize away.
The fusion result puts this bluntly, since the ensemble does not beat task 1 alone ($p = 1.0$).
Reporting the ensemble as the headline would overstate what multi-task fusion contributes; its
real value is insurance against a task like 7, which is at chance.

\textbf{What the representation costs, and what it buys.} On this same database, Drot\'{a}r et
al. report 81.3\% accuracy, 87.4\% sensitivity and 80.9\% specificity with a support vector
machine over handcrafted kinematic and pressure descriptors \cite{Drotar2016}. Our subject-level figures
range from 0.6901 macro F1 averaged over repeats to 0.7321 pooled. Reading pixels currently
costs accuracy relative to reading the signal, and the gap should be stated rather than left for
the reader to find.

Its size depends on which figure is compared: about eight points against our pooled 0.7321,
about twelve against the per-repeat average. It may be narrower than either, since the reference
is itself the product of a less conservative protocol, as noted below, and carries no reported
confidence interval.

The aggregate comparison also hides a reversal. Per task, \cite{Drotar2016} report 62.8\%
accuracy on the Archimedean spiral, obtained from pressure features alone because no kinematic
feature reached significance on that task. Our figure for the same task on the same database is
0.7194 balanced accuracy averaged over repeats, 0.7361 pooled, about nine points above. The
comparison is closer to like-for-like than the aggregate one: same task, same cohort, single
task on both sides, and neither figure benefits from task fusion. Two differences still favour
us and should be noted, namely that their task-1 subset has 69 subjects against our 72, and that
their filter was computed on the labelled corpus. The nested selection of
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} adds that the spiral is chosen in all 25 outer folds, so its standing
here is not an artefact of preselection.

Two properties of that comparison are worth making explicit. Neither work uses demographic or
clinical covariates: sex, age, disease duration, UPDRS-V and levodopa equivalent dose appear in
\cite{Drotar2016} as cohort description, and the twenty most relevant features listed there are
all derived from the handwriting itself. On that axis the comparison is fair. The evaluation
protocols, however, are not equally conservative. Features in \cite{Drotar2016} were retained by
a Mann--Whitney $U$ filter computed between the PD and control groups, applied to the labelled
corpus and not, as described, recomputed inside each cross-validation fold; the reported figure would
then carry the selection bias that nested inner-fold selection is designed to avoid. We raise
this to calibrate the gap, not to dispute the finding.

What that buys is a pipeline that never opens the \texttt{.svc} file after generation: no
kinematic descriptor designed, no pressure statistic chosen, no covariate admitted. Everything
downstream operates on an ordinary PNG, and the classifiers used here are a stock texture
descriptor and a stock ResNet. The ablation of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} was possible only
because the representation, not the feature set, is the object under test; a feature-engineered
pipeline cannot be ablated this way, since removing a pressure descriptor removes a number
rather than a property of the input.

Task 1 illustrates the practical consequence. Drot\'{a}r et al. found no statistically
significant kinematic feature for the Archimedean spiral and excluded it from their fused model,
attributing this to the absence of spiral-specific features in their set \cite{Drotar2016}. The
same task is the strongest single source of signal in our evaluation, at 0.7336 macro F1 and
0.7855 ROC AUC (Table~\ref{tab:ensemble}). A descriptor set has to be designed for the task it
will meet; a representation does not.

Explanation is where the difference is sharpest. Attribution over a handcrafted vector points at
a coordinate of that vector; over an abstract signal-to-image encoding such as a recurrence plot
it points at a cell of a matrix with no physical referent. On a minimap it points at a place on
the page and, because each channel is bound to exactly one named attribute, at a specific
property of the pen: intensity in the red channel is pressure and nothing else. That property supports
stroke-level saliency and per-task heat maps, which a descriptor vector does not admit.

Two further consequences follow from working on pixels. A raster-level representation does not
have to be redesigned when the tablet, the sampling rate or the task inventory changes, whereas
a kinematic descriptor set generally does. And minimaps can be generated in quantity from
unlabelled handwriting, placing the sample-size limitation within reach of self-supervised
pretraining, which a fixed-length vector of designed features does not allow.

The gap to 81.3\% is therefore a measurement taken at the least favourable point of this
representation's development, against a figure obtained under a less conservative protocol. It
was reached with an off-the-shelf texture descriptor, an untuned linear classifier, no
pretraining, no architecture search and augmentation restricted to integer translation. Whether
it closes is an empirical question this paper does not settle.

\textbf{Threats to validity.} Tasks 1, 3 and 7 were preselected during exploratory analysis
\emph{on this same corpus}, so the fixed-descriptor results carry an optimistic bias the nested
outer folds do not remove. Section~\ref{sec:ablation} puts that bias at roughly 0.08 macro F1,
and the screening in Table~\ref{tab:screening}, covering all eight tasks without preselection,
is the more conservative estimate. The ablation contrast is not affected: its four variants share
the preselection, so the bias moves them together. The automated reproduction check of the
screening stage returned \texttt{outside\_descriptive\_tolerance}: under the frozen protocol the
fixed configuration reached 0.7062 macro F1 against the 0.7452 recorded during exploratory
screening, a shortfall of 0.0388. The source of the divergence has not been isolated. It runs
\emph{below} the reference rather than above it, so the reported figures are the more
conservative of the two, and the reference values were not used for tuning. The cohort is also small and
single-source --- 75 subjects, one database, one acquisition protocol --- so device- and
population-specific effects cannot be separated from the phenomenon, and there is no external
validation. Per-task scaling profiles are computed over all files of a task; being
label-agnostic they leak no supervision, but they do make the encoding of a sample depend weakly
on the composition of the task set, and a strictly training-fold-only variant remains to be
quantified. Finally, a cross-validated macro F1 on a research database says nothing about
clinical utility. This is exploratory research software for computational handwriting analysis.
It is not a diagnostic system, it has not been validated for clinical use, and it must not be
read as a substitute for clinical assessment.

\section{\uppercase{Conclusions and Future Work}}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\noindent
We proposed Pseudodynamic Handwriting Minimap Signatures, an encoding that renders an online
handwriting task as a single static RGB image whose colour channels carry pen pressure, altitude
and azimuth and whose stroke width varies with writing speed. It is deterministic, versioned,
numerically equivalent across CPU and GPU implementations down to the rendered pixel, and
normalized per task without reference to labels.

The evidence supports the hypothesis in the form in which it was stated. Under a frozen
descriptor, classifier, split and threshold, the complete encoding averages 0.6901 macro F1 over
repeats and reaches 0.7321 pooled, against 0.5597 for a rendering that keeps only trajectory and
pen contact; the paired subject-level gain of 0.1725 (95\% CI [0.059, 0.287]) survives
Holm--Bonferroni correction. The
pseudo-dynamic content accounts for most of the margin above chance in this evaluation.
Stronger claims are not supported. Neither encoded dimension is individually significant on this
cohort, the tasks contribute very unevenly, three of the eight were preselected on the same
corpus, and no external validation was performed.

One secondary finding is worth carrying forward. On this cohort, standard texture descriptors
followed by a linear model outperformed a fine-tuned ResNet-18 ($\Delta = -0.172$, Holm-adjusted
$p = 0.040$). Semantic channel encodings and end-to-end deep learning sit in tension: the
semantics that make the representation auditable also forbid most of the augmentation deep
models need at this sample size.

Several directions follow. Additional online handwriting databases would separate the
representation from the idiosyncrasies of PaHaW and would remove the preselection bias, since
task selection could then be transferred rather than repeated. A third encoding iteration should
be driven by the interaction observed here rather than by intuition, testing alternative channel
assignments and stroke-width functions under the same factorial protocol. Minimaps are ordinary
images and can be generated in quantity from unlabelled handwriting, so self-supervised
pretraining is a direct way to address the sample-size limitation identified here, and worth
testing before concluding that convolutional models are unsuited to this representation. The explicit channel semantics also make attribution unusually
interpretable, since a saliency map over the red channel is a statement about pressure rather
than about an anonymous feature. Building a validated attribution instrument on top of that
property, on a model strong enough to be worth interrogating, is the natural continuation.
