\keywords{Handwriting Analysis, Visual Signatures, Signal-to-Image Encoding, Pseudo-Dynamic
Minimaps, Representation Learning, Online Handwriting, Parkinson's Disease.}

\abstract{Online handwriting acquired with a digitizing tablet carries dynamic information ---
pen pressure, altitude, azimuth, instantaneous speed and pen-up trajectories --- that is
discarded when the signal is rendered as a conventional binary image. Exploiting it normally
requires handcrafted kinematic descriptors or sequence models, both of which prevent direct
reuse of the computer vision toolchain. This paper proposes \emph{Pseudodynamic Handwriting
Minimap Signatures}, a formally specified encoding that maps an online handwriting signal onto a
single static RGB raster in which colour channels carry pen attributes and stroke width varies
with writing speed. The encoding is deterministic, versioned, CPU/GPU-equivalent, and
normalized per task in a label-agnostic way. It is evaluated on 597 minimaps from the 75
subjects of the PaHaW database under repeated subject-grouped cross-validation. A $2\times2$
factorial ablation isolates the contribution of the pseudo-dynamic content: the full encoding
reaches 0.7321 macro F1 against 0.5597 for a position-only rendering, a paired subject-level
gain of 0.1725 that survives Holm correction. A fine-tuned ResNet-18 performed significantly
worse than standard texture descriptors on this cohort. This is exploratory research software
for computational handwriting analysis and is not a diagnostic system.}

\section{\uppercase{Introduction}}
\label{sec:introduction}
\noindent
Handwriting is acquired in two fundamentally different regimes. \emph{Offline} acquisition
produces a static image: a scanned page, a photograph, a binary raster of ink. \emph{Online}
acquisition, performed with a digitizing tablet or an instrumented pen, produces a time-ordered
sequence of samples in which each point carries not only its planar coordinates but also the
pressure exerted on the surface, the inclination and rotation of the pen, and an implicit
velocity derived from consecutive timestamps. The online signal is strictly richer, and the
additional channels are precisely the ones that encode motor control rather than orthography.

This richness creates a representation problem. A computer vision pipeline expects an image. An
online handwriting signal is a multivariate time series. Reconciling the two has historically
been done in one of two ways, and both have costs. The first is to discard the temporal
dimension and rasterize the trajectory into a binary image, which throws away exactly the
attributes that motivated online acquisition. The second is to summarize the signal into a
vector of handcrafted kinematic and pressure descriptors --- stroke duration, number of changes
in velocity, pressure statistics --- and feed that vector to a conventional classifier
\cite{Drotar2016,Impedovo2019}. This second route retains dynamic information but replaces
representation learning with feature engineering: the descriptors must be designed, justified,
and re-designed for every new task or acquisition device.

A third route is possible: re-encode the dynamic signal \emph{into} the image, so that the image
itself becomes the carrier of the dynamics. This is the idea behind \emph{pseudo\-dynamic}
representations, and it is the subject of this paper. The proposed encoding, called a
\emph{Pseudodynamic Handwriting Minimap Signature}, renders an online handwriting task as a
single static RGB raster in which the three colour channels transport pen pressure, pen altitude
and pen azimuth, and the stroke width varies with the instantaneous writing speed.
Pen-up movements, which are invisible in a conventional rasterized sample and are known to be
informative \cite{Diaz2019}, are rendered as dashed light-gray segments. The output is an
ordinary image file. Any classifier that accepts images --- texture descriptors followed by a
linear model \cite{Cortes1995}, or a convolutional network \cite{LeCun1998,He2016} --- can
consume it directly.

Reducing the problem to an image classification problem is not merely a matter of convenience.
Four practical advantages follow directly. First, \emph{tooling reuse}: decades of texture
descriptors, convolutional architectures, attribution methods, data-loading infrastructure and
evaluation practice apply unchanged, without a single line of signal-specific code. Second,
\emph{transferability}: a representation defined at the level of pixels does not need to be
redesigned when the acquisition device, the sampling rate or the task inventory changes, whereas
a kinematic descriptor set generally does. Third, \emph{transfer learning}: models pretrained on
large natural-image corpora, and the self-supervised objectives developed for them, become
available to a domain in which labelled data is scarce and expensive to collect. Fourth,
\emph{visual auditability}: unlike a feature vector, an image can be inspected. A clinician or a
reviewer can look at the representation the model consumed, compare two subjects side by side,
and form an opinion about whether the encoding is capturing motor behaviour or an artifact ---
a property this paper exploits in Section~\ref{sec:tool}.

The term \emph{minimap} is borrowed from a line of work in which the same principle was applied
to a very different domain. Gebara Jr. et al. showed that the zoomed-out visual rendering of a
source file behaves as a machine-readable signature from which software properties can be
predicted \cite{Gebara2025}, and subsequently deployed this idea as a multi-IDE analysis
infrastructure \cite{Gebara2026}. Those minimaps are grayscale renderings of static text
artifacts. The present work extends the minimap principle in two directions simultaneously: the
source is a temporally sampled signal rather than a static artifact, and the encoding is
multi-channel and semantic rather than grayscale, with each channel assigned to a named physical
attribute of the pen.

The central hypothesis under test is the following:

\begin{quote}
Pseudodynamic Handwriting Minimap Signatures are a valid representation of online handwriting for
computer vision classifiers.
\end{quote}

\noindent Validity is understood here in a deliberately modest sense. The claim is not that the
encoding is optimal, nor that it is lossless in an information-theoretic sense. The claim is
that the pseudo-dynamic content survives the transformation and is measurably exploited by
generic image classifiers. That claim is directly falsifiable, and this paper tests it with a
$2\times2$ factorial ablation that switches the semantic colour channels and the speed-driven
stroke width on and off independently while holding the descriptor, the classifier, the
cross-validation splits and the decision threshold fixed.

The contributions of this paper are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A formal, versioned and reproducible encoding, Minimap Encoding V2, that maps an online
      handwriting signal to a static RGB image using label-agnostic per-task robust
      normalization (Section~\ref{sec:minimaps}).
\item A validation protocol establishing that the encoding is deterministic and numerically
      equivalent across CPU and GPU implementations (Section~\ref{sec:validation}).
\item A factorial ablation quantifying how much of the classification signal is attributable to
      the pseudo-dynamic content rather than to trajectory geometry
      (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
\item An interactive visualization tool that replays the original task execution and exposes its
      mapping to the minimap, including synchronized side-by-side comparison of two subjects on
      the same task (Section~\ref{sec:tool}).
\item An honest comparison between standard texture descriptors and a fine-tuned convolutional
      network on this representation, including a negative result for the latter
      (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:related} positions the work. Section~\ref{sec:minimaps} specifies the encoding
and the inspection tool. Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the experimental protocol,
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports results, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses them, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{\uppercase{Related Work}}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Minimaps as Visual Signatures}
\noindent
The immediate precedent for this work treats a rendered artifact not as a visualization intended
for a human reader but as a machine-readable signature intended for a model. Gebara Jr. et al.
generated minimaps of source files --- compact, zoomed-out, essentially illegible renderings of
the text --- and showed that convolutional models applied to those images can detect software
features without tokenization or syntactic parsing \cite{Gebara2025}. The follow-up work
packaged the approach as an ecosystem of IDE plugins backed by a decoupled inference service,
generating anonymized $128 \times 128$ grayscale minimaps per source file and validating three
prediction targets --- artifact type, project and author --- as feasibility benchmarks
\cite{Gebara2026}.

The same conviction underlies CV4Code \cite{Shi2022}, which encodes a code snippet as a
two-dimensional ASCII codepoint image and learns directly from it, explicitly avoiding lexical
and syntactic analysis and thereby becoming language-agnostic. What these works establish is
that a visual signature can be an adequate substrate for classification even when the source
domain is not visual at all.

Two properties of that prior art matter here. First, the source artifacts are \emph{static}: a
source file has no temporal ordering to preserve. Second, the encodings are \emph{grayscale}:
one intensity dimension, carrying essentially a density or codepoint value. Online handwriting
violates the first property, since the signal is a time series, and offers an opportunity to
relax the second, since several physically distinct attributes are measured simultaneously and
can be assigned to separate colour channels. The encoding proposed here addresses both points.

\subsection{Handwriting Analysis for Neurodegenerative Disease}
\noindent
Handwriting has been extensively studied as a behavioural marker of neurodegenerative
conditions, and Parkinson's disease in particular, because bradykinesia, rigidity and tremor
manifest directly in the fine motor control of the pen. Impedovo and Pirlo survey the field from
a pattern recognition standpoint and organize the literature around the dynamic descriptors
extracted from online acquisitions \cite{Impedovo2019}.

The PaHaW database \cite{Drotar2016} is the reference resource in this line. Its authors
extracted conventional kinematic descriptors together with newly proposed pressure descriptors
and compared $k$-nearest neighbours, AdaBoost and support vector machines, with the support
vector machine performing best. The relevant observation for the present work is that the
pipeline is entirely feature-engineered: performance depends on which descriptors were designed,
not on what a model extracted from the signal.

Learned representations have also been applied. Pereira et al. used convolutional networks over
signals captured by an instrumented pen, reporting that learned features outperformed the raw
signals \cite{Pereira2016}. Moetesum et al. moved in the opposite direction, quantifying the
discriminative power of \emph{visual} attributes of graphomotor samples with convolutional
feature extractors \cite{Moetesum2019}.

The closest work to the present proposal is that of Diaz et al., who introduced
\emph{dynamically enhanced} static handwriting images \cite{Diaz2019}. Their representation
retains temporal and velocity information by drawing sample points rather than linking them, and
by adding pen-up strokes, and it outperformed both purely static and purely dynamic baselines on
PaHaW. The encoding proposed in this paper shares that motivation but differs in mechanism: it
does not rely on point density as an implicit velocity proxy, it assigns each pen attribute to
an explicitly named channel with a documented scaling rule, and it renders speed through a
bounded stroke-width function rather than through sample sparsity. That difference is what makes
the factorial ablation of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} possible: each encoded attribute can be
switched off independently.

\subsection{Signal-to-Image Encodings}
\noindent
Outside handwriting, converting a time series into an image in order to apply convolutional
models is an established strategy. Recurrence plots, introduced by Eckmann et al. to visualize
the recurrence structure of dynamical systems \cite{Eckmann1987}, were later used directly as
classifier inputs \cite{Hatami2018}. Wang and Oates proposed Gramian angular fields and Markov
transition fields, encoding temporal correlation and transition dynamics as image texture, and
showed competitive results across standard benchmarks \cite{Wang2015}.

These encodings are \emph{abstract}: the resulting image is a matrix of pairwise relations with
no spatial correspondence to the phenomenon that produced the signal. A recurrence plot of a
handwriting task does not look like handwriting. The minimap encoding takes the opposite stance.
It preserves the planar geometry of the trace, so the image remains recognizable as handwriting,
and it uses the remaining degrees of freedom --- colour and stroke width --- to carry the
non-spatial attributes. This retains a property the abstract encodings lose: a human expert can
inspect the representation and reason about it, which matters when the encoding must be trusted
in a sensitive application domain.

\subsection{Positioning}
\noindent
Summarizing, minimaps have been validated as visual signatures for static, textual artifacts
under a grayscale encoding. Signal-to-image methods have been validated for time series under
encodings that discard spatial correspondence. Handwriting-specific pseudo-dynamic images have
been validated under encodings that embed dynamics implicitly. What has not been tested is a
representation that is simultaneously spatially faithful, multi-attribute, semantically explicit
and formally specified, applied to a temporally sampled signal --- nor has the contribution of
each encoded attribute been isolated experimentally. That is the gap this paper addresses.

\section{\uppercase{Pseudodynamic Minimap Signatures}}
\label{sec:minimaps}

\subsection{Input Signal}
\noindent
The input is an online handwriting task recorded as a sequence of $N$ samples
$p_i = (x_i, y_i, t_i, s_i, \alpha_i, \beta_i, \pi_i)$, $i = 1 \ldots N$, where $(x_i, y_i)$ are
planar coordinates, $t_i$ is a timestamp in milliseconds, $s_i \in \{0,1\}$ indicates whether
the pen is on the surface, $\alpha_i$ is the azimuth, $\beta_i$ is the altitude and $\pi_i$ is
the pressure. Consecutive samples define segments; the encoding is defined per segment.

\subsection{Design Rationale}
\noindent
Four requirements drove the design. First, \emph{spatial fidelity}: the rendered trace must
remain geometrically faithful to the original trajectory, so that the image is still
recognizable as handwriting and remains interpretable by a human. Second, \emph{channel
disjointness}: each measured attribute is assigned to exactly one visual dimension, and no
visual dimension carries two attributes. This is what makes the encoding auditable --- an
anomaly observed in the red channel can only originate from pressure --- and it is also what
makes the ablation of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} interpretable. Third, \emph{separability from
the background}: no semantic value may be confused with the empty canvas. Fourth, \emph{label
independence}: no quantity used to build the image may depend on the class label of any sample,
otherwise the representation itself leaks supervision into the downstream evaluation.

The resulting assignment is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:encoding}. It is worth stating what the
encoding deliberately does \emph{not} do. Velocity is not written into any colour channel, and
pressure does not modulate stroke width. Earlier iterations of the design conflated these, which
made channel-level attribution impossible.

\begin{table}[h]
\caption{Minimap Encoding V2: assignment of pen attributes to visual dimensions.}
\label{tab:encoding} \centering
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|}
  \hline
  Visual element & Encoded attribute \\
  \hline
  Red channel & Pressure \\
  Green channel & Altitude \\
  Blue channel & Azimuth \\
  Stroke width & Writing speed (Eq.~\\ref{eq:width}) \\
  Width range & 1--8 px \\
  Background & White, RGB (255, 255, 255) \\
  Pen-up segments & Light-gray dashed line \\
  \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Semantic Range}
\noindent
All three semantic channels are mapped into the interval $[30, 220]$ rather than the full
$[0,255]$ range. The background remains pure white, $(255,255,255)$. This guarantees that a
semantic pixel is never numerically confused with the canvas, and that the extreme values of an
attribute do not saturate into either the background or pure black. The cost is a reduction of
the usable dynamic range by roughly one quarter, which is acceptable given that the source
attributes are quantized far more coarsely than 190 levels.

\subsection{Speed to Stroke Width}
\noindent
For a segment between samples $i$ and $i+1$, the instantaneous speed is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:speed}
v_i = \frac{\sqrt{(x_{i+1}-x_i)^2 + (y_{i+1}-y_i)^2}}{t_{i+1}-t_i} ,
\end{equation}

\noindent which is compressed logarithmically as $\tilde{v}_i = \log(1+v_i)$. Only strictly
positive time differences are used. Invalid, repeated or non-monotonic timestamps occur in real
acquisitions and are handled by a deterministic fallback: the segment inherits the previous
valid speed, or, when no previous valid speed exists, the median valid speed of the file. This
rule is fixed and stateless with respect to input order, which is what makes the whole pipeline
reproducible.

Normalization is computed per task over all selected files, and never per class:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:speednorm}
\hat{v}_i = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{\tilde{v}_i - q_{5}}{\max(q_{95} - q_{5}, \epsilon)},\, 0,\, 1\right),
\end{equation}

\noindent where $q_{5}$ and $q_{95}$ are the $5$th and $95$th percentiles of $\tilde{v}$ over the
task. The stroke width in pixels is then

\begin{equation}\label{eq:width}
w_i = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\mathrm{round}\!\left(8 - 7\hat{v}_i\right),\, 1,\, 8\right).
\end{equation}

\noindent Equation~\ref{eq:width} is a monotonically decreasing function of the normalized
writing speed $\hat{v}_i$: the width is driven by speed, and it shrinks as the pen moves faster.
Speeds at or below the $5$th percentile render at 8 px, speeds at or above the $95$th percentile
render at 1 px, a zero speed renders at 8 px after clipping, and intermediate speeds occupy the
range 2--7 px. Slow movement therefore appears as a thick stroke, which matches the intuition of
a hesitant or effortful trace and keeps the representation legible to a human inspector.

\subsection{Pressure, Altitude and Azimuth}
\noindent
Pressure is scaled per task using pen-down samples only, since pressure recorded while the pen
is in the air carries no meaning:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:red}
R_i = \mathrm{round}\!\left(30 + 190\,\mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{\pi_i - \pi_{q5}}{\max(\pi_{q95}-\pi_{q5}, \epsilon)},0,1\right)\right).
\end{equation}

\noindent Altitude and azimuth are mapped to the green and blue channels respectively under the
same task-level robust percentile scaling and into the same $[30,220]$ interval. Invalid values
are excluded when the scaling profile is computed and clipped at render time, so a corrupt
sample can distort at most a single segment and never the profile of an entire task.

\subsection{Pen-Up Segments}
\noindent
Segments recorded with $s_i = 0$ correspond to movements executed above the surface. They are
rendered as light-gray dashed lines. Their pressure is not interpreted, since it is not
meaningful, but their width still follows the speed rule of Equation~\ref{eq:width}, so
the temporal structure of the aerial movement remains visible. Retaining pen-up trajectories
follows the finding that they carry discriminative content \cite{Diaz2019}.

\subsection{Rendering Procedure}
\noindent
Algorithm~\ref{alg:render} states the procedure. The two-pass structure --- profile computation
followed by rendering --- is what enforces label independence: the profile is a function of the
task, not of the labels.

\begin{algorithm}[!h]
 \caption{Minimap Encoding V2 rendering.}
 \label{alg:render}
 \KwData{Samples $p_1 \ldots p_N$ of a task; task-level profile $P$}
 \KwResult{RGB minimap $M$}
 initialize $M$ to white RGB (255,255,255)\;
 \For{$i \leftarrow 1$ \KwTo $N-1$}{
   compute $v_i$ by Eq.~\ref{eq:speed}, applying the fallback rule if $t_{i+1} \le t_i$\;
   $w_i \leftarrow$ width by Eq.~\ref{eq:speednorm}--\ref{eq:width} using $P$\;
   \eIf{$s_i = 1$}{
     $R_i \leftarrow$ Eq.~\ref{eq:red}; $G_i, B_i \leftarrow$ altitude, azimuth scaled by $P$\;
     draw solid segment $(x_i,y_i)\!\rightarrow\!(x_{i+1},y_{i+1})$ with colour $(R_i,G_i,B_i)$ and width $w_i$\;
   }{
     draw dashed light-gray segment with width $w_i$\;
   }
 }
 \Return $M$\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Determinism and Numerical Validation}
\label{sec:validation}
\noindent
Because the encoding is intended to be a specification rather than an implementation detail, it
is stored as a versioned machine-readable configuration and every generated minimap is
accompanied by a manifest recording the configuration version, the source file and a SHA-256
checksum of the output.

The generation of the 597 minimaps used in this work (Section~\ref{sec:setup}) was validated
automatically. All 597 images passed the structural audit: no broken file, no residual reference
to a previous encoding version, and observed stroke widths spanning exactly the specified range
of 1 to 8 pixels. CPU and CUDA implementations were compared segment by segment on 80 task files
covering 8 tasks. The maximum absolute difference in rendered RGB values was exactly $0$, the
maximum absolute difference in normalized speed was $2.22 \times 10^{-16}$ --- one unit in the
last place of double precision --- and stroke widths matched on every segment. Equivalence
therefore holds at the level of the rendered pixels, which is the level that matters, since the
classifiers never see the intermediate floating-point values.

\subsection{Visual Inspection Tool}
\label{sec:tool}
\noindent
An encoding is only useful if it can be trusted, and a static specification is difficult to
audit. A web-based viewer was therefore built as part of this work, and it is what allowed the
encoding to be designed, debugged and sanity-checked in the first place.

Figure~\ref{fig:animated} shows the single-subject mode. A subject is selected, and the tool
replays each task of that subject as it was originally executed, incrementally rendering the
trace at $1\times$, $2\times$, $4\times$ or $8\times$ playback speed while displaying the point
count and the duration of each task. A pen-orientation indicator shows altitude and azimuth as
they evolve, which makes it possible to confirm visually that the green and blue channels track
the physical inclination of the pen rather than an artifact of the scaling.

Figure~\ref{fig:sidebyside} shows the comparison mode, in which two subjects performing the
\emph{same} task are replayed synchronously. This is the mode in which the discriminative
content of the representation becomes apparent to a human observer: the Archimedean spiral of
the two subjects shown differs in trace regularity and in the speed profile revealed by the
colour gradient, even though both traces are geometrically similar.

The viewer displays subject metadata --- age, sex, dominant hand, and clinical descriptors such
as the UPDRS-V score, the levodopa equivalent dose and the disease duration. It must be stated
explicitly that this metadata is shown for human inspection and dataset auditing only. It is
never provided to any model. As stated in Section~\ref{sec:setup}, the classifiers consume
minimap images, subject identifiers, task identifiers and labels, and nothing else.

The tool is a research and inspection instrument. It is not a clinical interface and is not
intended for use in patient assessment.

\section{\uppercase{Experimental Setup}}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Database and Generated Corpus}
\noindent
Experiments use the PaHaW database \cite{Drotar2016}, which contains online handwriting from 75
subjects --- 37 diagnosed with Parkinson's disease and 38 healthy controls --- each performing
eight tasks on a digitizing tablet. The tasks comprise an Archimedean spiral, repeated writing
of orthographically simple syllables and words, and the writing of a sentence. Access to PaHaW
requires a signed licence agreement; no data is redistributed with this work.

Each task is stored as an \texttt{.svc} file whose first line is the sample count and whose
remaining lines contain seven columns in the order

\begin{small}
\begin{verbatim}
 y  x  timestamp  state  azimuth  altitude
 pressure
\end{verbatim}
\end{small}

\noindent with \texttt{state} equal to 1 for pen-on-surface and 0 for pen-in-air. Note that the
$y$ coordinate precedes $x$; this ordering is unusual and is a recurrent source of reproduction
errors when the format is read under the more common $(x,y)$ assumption.

Generation under encoding version 2.0 produced \textbf{597 minimaps} of $512 \times 512$ pixels
in RGB, with no file skipped and no failure. Coverage is complete for tasks 2 to 8, at 75 images
each; task 1 yields 72 images because three subjects have no usable recording for it.

\subsection{Information Available to the Classifiers}
\noindent
The \texttt{.svc} files are used exclusively to render minimaps. After generation, every
classifier and every analysis in this work consumes only four things: the minimap image, the
subject identifier used for grouping, the task identifier used for organization, and the
H/PD label used as the target. No handcrafted kinematic or pressure descriptor is extracted from
the raw signal, no demographic or clinical covariate is used, and no fusion of dynamic and
visual modalities is performed. This restriction is deliberate: it is what makes the experiment
a test of the \emph{representation} rather than of a pipeline built around it.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\noindent
All splits are subject-independent. Outer evaluation uses stratified group $k$-fold with 5 folds
repeated 5 times, grouped by subject identifier, so no subject ever appears simultaneously in
training and evaluation. Model, descriptor and fusion decisions are taken inside 3 inner grouped
folds, never using outer-test subjects. The random seed is 42 and the decision threshold is
fixed at 0.50. Confidence intervals are percentile bootstrap over 10,000 subject-level
resamplings, and paired comparisons use paired subject bootstrap, paired permutation tests,
exact McNemar tests and Holm--Bonferroni correction. The frozen split checksum is
\texttt{ca8d48f4\ldots a6bf5dcf}.

The primary metric is macro F1, reported with balanced accuracy, per-class recall, Matthews
correlation coefficient and ROC AUC. Because the cohort is almost perfectly balanced, a
classifier that always predicts the majority class attains a balanced accuracy of exactly 0.500
by construction; that value is the reference against which all results below should be read.

\subsection{Evaluated Pipelines}
\noindent
Three pipelines were evaluated, all consuming only minimap pixels.

\textbf{Texture-descriptor screening.} A nested evaluation over all eight tasks explored ten
descriptor families --- grayscale LBP, RGB semantic-channel LBP, opponent LBP, LPQ,
GLCM/Haralick, Gabor, HOG, wavelet texture, semantic channel statistics, and raster-derived
stroke-width texture --- against logistic regression, linear and RBF support vector machines
\cite{Cortes1995}, random forests and extra trees. Descriptor, classifier and task-fusion
settings were chosen inside inner folds only.

\textbf{Fixed descriptor with reliability-weighted fusion.} The configuration retained from
screening, denoted \texttt{lpq\_rgb\_visual\_geometry\_width}, concatenates LPQ computed
independently on the R, G and B channels (768 features, window size 5, Hellinger-transformed
histograms, after fixed foreground crop, 5\% margin, white padding and $64 \times 64$ bilinear
resize) with 44 visual geometry and stroke-width features computed on the unresized PNG, for 812
features in total. The classifier is a fixed pipeline of median imputation, standardization and
logistic regression with $C = 0.1$, L2 penalty, balanced class weights and the liblinear solver.
Per-task predictions for tasks 1, 3 and 7 are fused with reliability weights
$\max(\text{inner macro F1} - 0.5,\, 0.01)$, renormalized over the tasks available for each
subject.

\textbf{Direct convolutional classification.} A ResNet-18 \cite{He2016} is fine-tuned on the raw
minimap pixels under identical splits, class weighting, threshold and fusion rule. Because the
RGB channels are semantic, the augmentations normally used for natural images --- rotation,
scaling, blur, colour jitter, crops, flips, erasing, MixUp, CutMix --- would corrupt the encoded
attributes and are excluded. The only train-time augmentation is a safe canvas translation
applied on the $512 \times 512$ raster before resizing: integer-pixel copy with white fill, no
interpolation, maximum shift of 16 pixels on each axis, probability 0.80, with foreground
clipping forbidden. An audit of 1046 training-time transformations recorded 851 translated and
195 identity samples, with zero clipped samples and zero foreground-preservation failures.

\subsection{Factorial Ablation}
\label{sec:ablation-setup}
\noindent
To isolate the contribution of the pseudo-dynamic content, four rendering variants were
generated from the same signals in a $2\times2$ factorial design crossing semantic colour with
speed-driven stroke width. \textsc{Mono\_Fixed} keeps only position, trajectory and pen contact,
as a monochrome fixed-width line; \textsc{Rgb\_Fixed} adds the semantic colour channels;
\textsc{Mono\_Speed} adds speed-driven stroke width; and \textsc{Rgb\_Speed\_V2} is the
complete encoding. Descriptor, classifier, splits and threshold are held fixed across all four,
so any difference is attributable to the representation alone. Pen-contact information is
present in every variant, including the minimal one.

\subsection{Convolutional Network on the Same Representation}
\noindent
The fine-tuned ResNet-18 with safe translation reaches 0.5601 macro F1 at the ensemble level
(95\% CI [0.4444, 0.6701]), balanced accuracy 0.5711, PD recall 0.4054, MCC 0.1509 and ROC AUC
0.6024. On task 1 it reaches 0.5625 macro F1 with a strongly asymmetric operating point: H
recall 0.9444 against PD recall 0.2778.

Paired against the texture pipeline on the same subjects and splits, the convolutional model is
\emph{worse}, not better: $\Delta = -0.1720$ at the ensemble level (95\% CI [$-0.294$,
$-0.054$], permutation $p = 0.010$, exact McNemar $p = 0.017$, Holm-adjusted $p = 0.040$). The
gap is also significant on task 1 before correction ($\Delta = -0.1711$, permutation
$p = 0.033$) and not significant on tasks 3 and 7. This is a negative result and is reported as
such: on a cohort of this size, standard texture descriptors extract the pseudo-dynamic content
of the minimaps more effectively than end-to-end fine-tuning does.

\subsection{Channel Attribution}
\noindent
Gradient-based attribution over the convolutional model, aggregated per semantic channel,
assigns a mean relative importance of 0.406 to the green channel (altitude), 0.352 to the red
channel (pressure) and 0.243 to the blue channel (azimuth); the bootstrap interval for the red
channel is [0.335, 0.368]. The ordering is stable across tasks 1, 3 and 7. Attribution is higher
on green and lower on blue for PD subjects (0.423 and 0.232) than for controls (0.389 and
0.253). These figures are exploratory: they describe where a weaker model looked, not which
attribute carries the discriminative signal, and no causal reading is intended.

\subsection{Relation to a Clipping-Based Pipeline}
\noindent
For context, a convolutional pipeline that operates on binary stroke clippings rather than on a
pseudo-dynamic encoding \needcite{the clipping-based CNN baseline; it currently exists only as an
unpublished undergraduate thesis and an accompanying public repository, so an appropriate
citable form must be decided} reports 69.23\% task-level accuracy and 66.67\% F1 on PaHaW task 2
with 62 training and 13 test subjects. That pipeline extracts one square clipping per coordinate
point --- roughly 83{,}955 clippings for the task --- and aggregates clipping-level predictions
upward into stroke-, letter- and task-level decisions.

The two numbers must not be placed in the same table. The protocols differ in task, in metric,
in split design and, decisively, in test-set size: a single held-out split of 13 subjects moves
by 7.7 accuracy points per subject, whereas the results above use 25 subject-grouped evaluation
rounds with bootstrap intervals. The clipping pipeline is cited here to characterize the
regime, not to be outranked. One property of it is worth recording, however: its clipping-level
test accuracy stayed in the 50--52\% band throughout training while training accuracy reached
96.8\%, the expected consequence of treating tens of thousands of overlapping crops from a few
dozen writers as independent samples.

\section{\uppercase{Discussion}}
\label{sec:discussion}

\noindent
\textbf{The hypothesis survives its own falsification test.} The ablation was designed so that
the encoding could fail: if the colour channels and the speed-driven width carried nothing, the
minimal position-only rendering would have matched the full one. It did not. \textsc{Mono\_Fixed}
sits at 0.5597 macro F1, barely above the 0.500 balanced accuracy of a trivial classifier, and
the complete encoding sits at 0.7321. The gain is paired at the subject level, survives Holm
correction, and its confidence interval excludes zero. Since descriptor, classifier, splits and
threshold were frozen across the four variants, the difference is attributable to the
representation and to nothing else. That is the central empirical claim of this paper.

\textbf{Neither factor is sufficient alone.} No individual main effect reached significance
after correction, yet their combination did. Read together with the observation that each factor
is worth roughly twice as much in the presence of the other, the most plausible account is that
the two dimensions are complementary rather than redundant: stroke width localizes \emph{where}
the movement slowed, and the colour channels describe \emph{how the pen was held and pressed}
while it slowed. Neither alone reconstructs the motor event. This reading is consistent with the
descriptor screening, where the only task whose selected descriptor combined colour and
stroke-width texture is also the only task that clearly exceeded chance individually.

\textbf{Why the convolutional model lost.} The negative CNN result is not evidence against the
representation --- both pipelines consume the same pixels. It is evidence about sample size.
With 597 images from 75 subjects, and with the semantic RGB channels forbidding every standard
photometric and geometric augmentation, a fine-tuned ResNet-18 has orders of magnitude more
parameters than the cohort can constrain. Its operating point on task 1 makes the failure mode
explicit: 0.944 recall on controls against 0.278 on patients, a model that has learned to
predict the majority side of its decision boundary. The augmentation designed for this setting
was deliberately conservative --- integer translation only, audited for foreground preservation
--- precisely because anything stronger would have destroyed the encoded attributes. That
constraint is intrinsic to semantic channel encodings and deserves to be stated as a design
cost: the richer the semantics of the channels, the smaller the space of admissible
augmentations.

\textbf{Task heterogeneity.} Task 1, the Archimedean spiral, dominates. It is a continuous
ballistic movement with no orthographic content, executed against a fixed template, which makes
speed and pressure irregularities directly comparable across subjects. Tasks with repeated
lifts, restarts and letter shapes introduce variance that the representation does not normalize
away. The fusion result quantifies this bluntly: the ensemble does not beat task 1 alone
($p = 1.0$). Reporting the ensemble as the headline number would therefore overstate what
multi-task fusion contributes; its actual value is insurance against a task like 7, which is at
chance.

\textbf{Threats to validity.} Five should be stated plainly. First, tasks 1, 3 and 7 were
preselected during exploratory analysis \emph{on this same corpus}, so the fixed-descriptor
results carry an optimistic bias that the nested outer folds do not remove; the screening in
Table~\ref{tab:screening}, which covers all eight tasks without preselection, is the more
conservative estimate. Second, the automated reproduction check of the screening stage returned
\texttt{outside\_descriptive\_tolerance}, meaning the fixed configuration did not exactly
reproduce the exploratory reference metrics; the outer-fold results were not tuned to close that
gap, but the discrepancy is unexplained and should be resolved. Third, the cohort is small and
single-source: 75 subjects, one database, one acquisition protocol, so device- and
population-specific effects cannot be separated from the phenomenon, and no external validation
was performed. Fourth, the per-task scaling profiles are computed over all files of a task;
although label-agnostic, and therefore free of supervision leakage, they make the encoding of a
sample depend weakly on the composition of the task set, and a strictly training-fold-only
variant remains to be quantified. Fifth, and most importantly, a cross-validated macro F1 on a
research database says nothing about clinical utility. This work is exploratory research
software for computational handwriting analysis. It is not a diagnostic system, it has not been
validated for clinical use, and it must not be read as a substitute for clinical assessment.

\section{\uppercase{Conclusions and Future Work}}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\noindent
This paper proposed Pseudodynamic Handwriting Minimap Signatures, a formally specified encoding
that renders an online handwriting task as a single static RGB image in which colour channels
carry pen pressure, altitude and azimuth, and stroke width varies with writing speed. The
encoding is deterministic, versioned, numerically equivalent across CPU and GPU implementations
down to the rendered pixel, and normalized per task in a label-agnostic manner.

Regarding the hypothesis under test, the evidence supports it in the form in which it was
stated. Under a frozen descriptor, classifier, split and threshold, the complete encoding
reaches 0.7321 macro F1 against 0.5597 for a rendering that keeps only trajectory and pen
contact, a paired subject-level gain of 0.1725 (95\% CI [0.059, 0.287]) that survives
Holm--Bonferroni correction. The pseudo-dynamic content is therefore not decorative: it is the
larger part of what the classifier uses. The evidence does not support stronger claims. Neither
encoded dimension is individually significant on this cohort, the tasks contribute very
unevenly, three of the eight tasks were preselected on the same corpus, and no external
validation was performed.

A secondary finding is worth carrying forward. On this representation and this cohort, standard
texture descriptors followed by a linear model significantly outperformed a fine-tuned
ResNet-18 ($\Delta = -0.172$, Holm-adjusted $p = 0.040$). Semantic channel encodings and
end-to-end deep learning are in tension: the semantics that make the representation auditable
also forbid most of the augmentation that deep models need at this sample size.

Four directions follow. Evaluating the encoding on additional online handwriting databases would
separate the representation from the idiosyncrasies of PaHaW and would remove the preselection
bias, since task selection could then be transferred rather than repeated. A third encoding
iteration should be driven by the interaction observed here rather than by intuition, testing
alternative channel assignments and stroke-width functions under the same factorial protocol.
Because minimaps are ordinary images and can be generated in quantity from unlabelled
handwriting, self-supervised pretraining addresses the sample-size limitation directly and is
the most promising route to making convolutional models competitive on this representation.
Finally, the explicit semantics of the channels make attribution unusually interpretable: a
saliency map over the red channel is a statement about pressure, not about an anonymous feature.
Turning the exploratory channel attribution reported here into a validated instrument, on a
model strong enough to be worth interrogating, is the natural continuation.
